Why do computers only use 0 and 1?

Your voice is a smooth wave with infinite values, yet your phone keeps only zeros and ones. These notes explain why that is not a downgrade, and why it is the reason digital won.

Video 7:44Read 8 minBeginnerExams · GATE · viva
▶ Watch the episodeDigital Electronics #1Animated, 7:44. Watch first, then use these notes to revise.

In 30 seconds

  • An analog signal can take any value. Its exact height is the information, so any noise corrupts it.
  • Digital only asks “high or low?”. Small noise changes nothing, and every gate rebuilds clean levels.
  • Two levels give the biggest noise gap and match a transistor’s natural ON/OFF. Use many bits for detail: n bits give 2n values.

01What “analog” really means

Turn a fan regulator slowly and the speed changes smoothly, with no jumps. That is analog: a signal that can take any value in a range. Your voice works the same way. Air pressure rises and falls in a smooth wave, and a microphone turns it into a voltage with the same shape.

At one instant the voltage might be 2.3 V, a moment later 2.31 V, then 2.314 V. Every tiny change carries meaning: the exact height of the wave is the information.

A light switch is different. It has only two positions, on or off. That is the digital idea: instead of infinite values, allow only two, and call them 1 and 0.

02The noise problem

Every wire, amplifier and circuit adds a tiny random wobble to the signal. Heat causes it, nearby wires cause it, and you cannot switch it off. In an analog signal that is a disaster: noise changes the height, so it changes the information. Worse, the receiver cannot tell which part is signal and which part is noise. They are mixed together for good.

Chinese whispers. Each person repeats the sentence with a small error, and by the tenth person it is nonsense. Analog signals play this game every time they are amplified, copied or transmitted. That is why a cassette copied from a copy sounded worse every time.

03The digital trick: high or low?

Digital circuits never read the exact voltage. They ask one question: is it high, or is it low? For a classic 5 V CMOS chip, anything above about 3.5 V reads as a 1, and anything below about 1.5 V reads as a 0. The space in between is the forbidden zone, and a healthy signal never stays there.

The real magic: regeneration

The next gate does not just read the 1. It sends out a fresh, clean 5 V again. So every stage throws the noise away and restores perfect levels. That is Chinese whispers where every person repeats the sentence perfectly, and it is why the millionth copy of a digital file is identical to the first.

Exam corner: noise margins. The voltage ranges above have names. VIH is the lowest input voltage still read as 1 (about 3.5 V here). VIL is the highest input still read as 0 (about 1.5 V). A gate’s outputs are VOH (near 5 V) and VOL (near 0 V). The noise margins are NMH = VOH − VIH and NML = VIL − VOL: how much noise a signal can take before it is misread. Exact values come from each chip’s datasheet.

04Why exactly two levels?

If two levels are good, why not ten, to carry more information per wire? Because ten levels squeezed into the same 5 V leave tiny gaps between them, and a little noise can push a 3 into a 4. That is the analog problem again.

  • Biggest safety gap. Two levels put the most possible distance between 0 and 1.
  • Transistors are natural switches. Fully on or fully off: two states, for free.

Many bits for rich signals

To describe something rich like your voice, use many bits together. Each extra bit doubles the number of values: n bits give 2n values.

Sampling and quantization

A music CD measures the sound wave 44,100 times every second and stores each measurement as a 16-bit number. Measuring at fixed instants is sampling. Rounding each measurement to the nearest allowed level is quantization. The chip that does both is an ADC (analog-to-digital converter); a DAC turns the numbers back into a voltage for your speaker.

Is anything lost? A little: the rounding (quantization) error never fully goes away. With 16 bits it is far below what ears can notice. Stereo CD audio is 44,100 × 16 × 2 = 1,411,200 bits per second, about 1.4 Mbit/s.

The real world stays analog. Digital lives in the middle, with converters at both ends: microphone → ADC → digital processing → DAC → speaker.

05Key terms

Analog signal
Can take any value in a continuous range.
Digital signal
Takes only a fixed set of levels; in binary, just 0 and 1.
Noise
Random unwanted variation added by heat, nearby wires and every circuit.
VIH / VIL
Input thresholds: at or above VIH reads 1, at or below VIL reads 0.
Forbidden zone
Between VIL and VIH; the reading is not guaranteed.
Regeneration
Each gate outputs fresh, clean levels, so noise never builds up.
Bit
One binary digit, 0 or 1. n bits give 2n values.
Sampling
Measuring a signal at fixed instants (CD: 44,100 per second).
Quantization
Rounding each sample to the nearest allowed level.
ADC / DAC
Converters: analog to digital, and digital back to analog.

06Practice check

1. A 5 V CMOS input reads 4.2 V. What does the chip see?

  1. 0
  2. 1
  3. The forbidden zone
  4. It depends on the last value
Show answerB, 1. 4.2 V is above the 3.5 V threshold (VIH).

2. Why does a digital signal survive many copies better than an analog one?

  1. It uses higher voltage
  2. Every stage restores clean levels
  3. It uses less power
  4. It travels faster
Show answerB. Each stage throws the noise away and regenerates perfect 0s and 1s.

3. How many different values can 8 bits represent?

  1. 8
  2. 16
  3. 255
  4. 256
Show answerD, 256. 28 = 256. 255 is only the largest value, counting from 0.

Viva: Why don’t computers use ten voltage levels per wire?

Show answerTen levels in the same voltage range leave tiny gaps, so small noise causes misreads. Two levels give the largest noise margin, and transistors naturally work as ON/OFF switches.

Viva: What is the difference between sampling and quantization?

Show answerSampling chooses when to measure (time becomes discrete). Quantization rounds each measurement to the nearest allowed level (amplitude becomes discrete).
